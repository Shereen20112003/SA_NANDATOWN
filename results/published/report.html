Many agents.
One point of failure.

Different identities are not necessarily independent alternatives. A controlled comparison of agent selection under shared backend outages.

The experiment

12 service agents. Nine share one backend; three use separate backends. Eight buyers submit 20 tasks each. Three attempts per task. Every request, response, reservation and payment is evaluated against the exported Nanda Town trace.

450 runs / 10 paired seeds
No LLM credentials, external services, or real money.

Seed-level intervals characterize this synthetic workload, not real-world reliability. An interval of zero width may reflect deterministic structure, not certainty about deployment.

The counterexample matters

In the staggered provider-outage control, stress-window completion is 69.4% for dependency-aware selection versus 80.0% for price selection. In clean operation the average successful task costs 13.000 cents versus 10.000 cents.

The candidate is not assumed to win every failure regime. Only explicitly reported backend failures affect group-level retry avoidance; provider-local failures exclude the individual provider, not its whole group.

What this does not prove

  • This is a seeded simulation with a shared reservation coordinator, not a decentralized deployment.
  • Dependency labels are truthful when present. Missing labels share one conservative unknown group. No inference, dishonest declarations, or multi-hop dependencies are modeled. Structured failure reasons are also assumed truthful, not inferred from timeouts.
  • The oracle knows dependency identities, not future outages. It is an information-rich comparator, not a proven optimum.
  • The independent control staggers nine provider outages with the same total provider downtime as the shared case. It does not match instantaneous unavailable capacity or outage exposure for every task.
  • Prices are synthetic. Only successful tasks are charged; failed attempts still consume messages and retry budget.
  • A passing evidence verdict means internal consistency, not a resilience certificate or verified real-world dependency truth.
